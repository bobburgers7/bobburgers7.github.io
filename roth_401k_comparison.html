▶ 🔬 Research Insights: When Are Roth Conversions Most Beneficial?

Based on research by Edward F. McQuarrie: "When and for Whom Are Roth Conversions Most Beneficial? A New Set of Guidelines, Cautions and Caveats"

Much has changed since penalty-free Roth conversions were inaugurated in 2010. Tax rates have gone up and down. The re-characterization provision went away. Heirs can no longer stretch out inherited Roth accounts over a lifetime. Medicare surcharges were expanded and began to adjust for inflation. The age to begin Required Minimum Distributions was pushed out to age 72 and the IRS changed the RMD divisor tables to further slow the pace of distribution.

📖 What Conventional Wisdom Says:

The rough consensus has been:

  1. Convert if tax rate is lower now than future RMDs: Intertemporal tax arbitrage—pay less tax now to avoid more tax later
  2. Don't convert if tax rate is higher now: Unless you fear tax rates will be hiked in the future
  3. Indifferent if tax rates are the same: Theory says after-tax wealth should be identical either way

⚠️ The Problem: In practice, motivated advisors can always recommend a conversion by arguing "taxes are going up!" Additionally, conventional wisdom claims paying conversion tax from outside funds (rather than from converted funds) produces superior results—but McQuarrie shows this involves many pitfalls and is not as beneficial as commonly believed.

📊 How Much Do You Need to Face High Tax Brackets?

McQuarrie notes that popular accounts often fail to show that multi-million-dollar balances are required just to reach middling tax brackets in retirement. This is because RMDs start small (based on life expectancy divisors) and grow gradually.

Example (2026 dollars, adjusted for inflation):
• To hit 22% bracket at age 75: Need ~$2M+ Traditional balance (RMD ≈ $73k + standard deduction = ~$87k income)
• To hit 24% bracket: Need ~$3.5M+ Traditional balance
• To hit 32% bracket: Need ~$8M+ Traditional balance

Note: Tax brackets adjust for inflation each year. This calculator properly inflates bracket boundaries, standard deductions, and thresholds throughout your retirement.

🔍 Key Findings That Challenge Conventional Wisdom:

  • Future tax rates need not be higher for a conversion to pay off
  • Paying conversion tax from outside funds is not as helpful as commonly believed
  • Not especially beneficial for top bracket taxpayers compared to middle class taxpayers
  • Greatest benefit: Taxpayers who can convert partly in the zero percent tax bracket (i.e., during a year with no other taxable income)

💰 The Tax Arbitrage Opportunity:

The research focuses on affluent professional couples—for example, those with peak earnings around $400,000 who faced marginal tax rates of 31-36% during their working years. The key insight: When one or both retire early (e.g., mid-60s), they often drop to much lower tax brackets before Social Security and RMDs begin.

📊 Example Arbitrage Scenario:
• While Working: Made all contributions to Traditional 401(k) at 31-36% marginal rate (saving $31-36 per $100 contributed)
• Early Retirement: Convert Traditional → Roth at 0-12% marginal rate (paying only $0-12 per $100 converted)
• Net Benefit: $19-36 tax savings per $100, plus decades of tax-free compounding

⏰ The Time Factor:

While the benefits from a Roth conversion are often small and slow to arrive, a Roth conversion will almost always pay off if given enough time—for life spans that extend past 90 and so long as annual distributions from converted amounts are not taken. Roth conversions work because of compounding, which requires the conversion to be left undisturbed for a long time.

💡 Bottom Line: The mathematics of compounding underwrite the success of Roth conversions. The longer you can leave converted amounts untouched, the more powerful the benefits become. The greatest opportunity exists for high earners who retire early and can convert during low-income gap years.

⏱️ Time Value of Money in Roth Conversions

McQuarrie emphasizes a critical point often overlooked: a dollar of tax paid today ≠ a dollar of tax paid 30 years from now. Intertemporal tax arbitrage means paying a lump sum tax NOW to reduce a stream of tax payments over DECADES (RMDs from age 75-90+).

✓ This Calculator's Approach: Every tax payment is compounded forward to age 90 at your expected return rate. This captures the opportunity cost—a $1,000 tax at age 60 represents $7,612 in lost wealth at age 90 (assuming 7% growth for 30 years). By expressing all taxes as "age 90 dollars," we properly account for when taxes are paid, not just how much.

🔢 The Math: Why RMDs Break the Indifference Theorem

The "indifference theorem" states that if tax rates are constant, Roth and Traditional accounts should produce identical after-tax wealth. Mathematically: $100 × (1-24%) × (1+7%)^30 = $100 × (1+7%)^30 × (1-24%)

This holds for lump-sum withdrawals. But Required Minimum Distributions break this equality because they force you to withdraw funds as a stream (annually from age 75-90+), not a lump sum. Each RMD withdrawal:

  • Reduces the compounding base in your Traditional account
  • Gets taxed, potentially at higher rates due to Social Security taxation and IRMAA surcharges
  • Limits flexibility - you must take distributions whether you need the money or not

✓ Roth Advantage: By converting during low-tax years (ages 59-74), you pay tax at favorable rates and then enjoy unlimited tax-free compounding with no RMDs. The full balance compounds uninterrupted, and withdrawals are completely tax-free and don't trigger Social Security taxation or IRMAA surcharges.

💎 The Power of Compounding: Why Roth Conversions Always Win in the Long Run

McQuarrie illustrates this with a striking thought experiment: If a Roman centurion at the time of Christ had invested one drachma at 4% for 2,000 years, what would it be worth? "A huge number." But what if a second centurion invested the same amount but paid 12% tax on interest each year, earning only 3.52%?

The critical insight: After 996 years, the first centurion would have 100× the wealth of the second. Put differently: one penny invested at 4% tax-free eventually equals one dollar invested at 3.52% taxed.

📐 The Math Principle:
• One-time tax hit (Roth conversion): Arithmetic reduction (pay 24% → start with 76% of amount)
• Ongoing tax drag (Traditional + RMDs): Exponential reduction (smaller balance compounds every year)
• Result: Given enough time, the exponential function (tax-free compounding) must overcome any initial deficit

Roth conversions left untouched always win—in the long run. Even a 99% conversion tax eventually gets beaten by tax-free compounding, given enough years. Changes in future tax rates only moderate the pace and scale of the win, not the eventual outcome.

⚠️ The Catch: This requires leaving the Roth untouched to compound and living long enough to reach the crossover point. For typical retirees (not Roman centurions), this usually means life spans extending into the late 80s or 90s. The calculator models this by comparing wealth at age 90.

⚙️ Calculator Methodology: This calculator addresses McQuarrie's key methodological concerns:

Inflation Adjustments: Tax bracket boundaries, standard deductions, IRMAA thresholds, and NIIT thresholds all inflate annually at your specified rate. This prevents false conclusions from comparing today's brackets to future income.

Consistent Return Assumptions: Both Roth and Traditional accounts use identical CAGR throughout the analysis. No hidden assumptions or different return profiles.

Time Value of Money: Every tax payment is compounded forward to age 90 to capture opportunity cost. A $10,000 tax at age 60 is treated as $76,123 in lost wealth at age 90 (at 7% CAGR).

Tax Payment Options: McQuarrie emphasizes that the base case must be paying tax from the converted funds themselves (reducing the Roth amount). This calculator offers two options:

1. "Converted Funds" (Recommended - McQuarrie Base Case): Tax is paid from the conversion, so converting $100k at 24% tax results in only $76k going into the Roth. This is the most conservative and realistic approach for most people.

2. "Taxable Account": The full conversion amount goes to Roth, and tax is paid from your taxable account. This requires large taxable account balances and, per McQuarrie, "virtually requires the existence of that large sum of taxable dollars sitting around doing nothing." This option may overstate benefits.

Both scenarios (Max Roth vs All Traditional) use identical assumptions for fair comparison: same asset returns, same tax payment method, and consistent treatment across all accounts.

Note on RMD Age: This calculator assumes an RMD age of 75, which applies to individuals born after December 31, 1959. If you were born earlier, your RMD age may be different:

  • Before July 1, 1949: RMD age 70½
  • July 1, 1949 to Dec. 31, 1950: RMD age 72
  • Jan. 1, 1951 to Dec. 31, 1959: RMD age 73
  • After Dec. 31, 1959: RMD age 75
